2. Official Challenge Requirements — Source-Derived

The brief asks for an Agentic Fraud Investigation Agent powered by TigerGraph. It describes investigating fraud,

creating/progressing cases, gathering evidence, handling uncertainty, requesting additional evidence, recommending/executing

permitted next-best actions, explaining reasoning, stopping when enough evidence exists, and using prior case memory.

Required components stated in the brief: TigerGraph Savanna or Community Edition; GSQL and graph algorithms;

TigerGraph MCP; GraphRAG; UI. The brief states that agent framework and LLM choice are optional, with the LLM supporting

reasoning, tool selection, evidence synthesis and explanations rather than replacing graph analysis.

Dataset facts stated in the brief: about 590,000 card transactions; about 13,500 customers; device/connection records;

transaction risk score; closed investigations for the first four months; bank fraud policy; five known fraud patterns; regulatory

references; 20 benchmark cases from the final two months; some fraud patterns may be undocumented.

Submission expectations: working agent; GitHub repo; outputs for 20 cases including investigation

record/evidence/findings/decisions/actions; case written to graph; SAR when required; next-best-action and approval route

before/after additional evidence; 3–5 minute demo; technical blog; social post.

Judging: Investigation accuracy 25%; Next best action 25%; Case summary/explainability 10%; Agentic design/engineering

15%; Innovation 15%; Demo quality 10%.

4. The Three Core Mechanisms

Step 1 — Investigation State

•
Case: target transaction/entity, case identity, status.

•
Hypotheses: competing explanations, supporting evidence, contradictions, unresolved questions and potential falsifiers.

•
Evidence: provenance, source/tool/query, factual observation, relationship/context where available, and hypothesis impact.

•
Unknowns: explicitly separate known, unknown, contradictory, unresolved and irrelevant.

•
Current decision state: sufficient/insufficient/other states only where supported by policy/data.

•
Investigation history: actions, evidence, rejected paths and reasons.

•
Budget/bounds: proposed safeguards against repeated or endless investigation.

Rule: the LLM interprets evidence; it must not become the source of factual graph relationships.

Step 2 — Choosing the Next Evidence

•
Generate candidate investigations.

•
Connect each candidate to a current uncertainty or competing hypothesis.

•
Estimate expected decision impact: could the result materially change the decision?

•
Account for justified investigation cost/constraints.

•
Make selection explainable: why A over B/C?

•
Ensure the next step depends on what was discovered rather than being a fixed script.

Step 3 — Knowing When to Stop

•
Can current evidence justify a permitted decision?

•
Is there unresolved uncertainty that could materially change that decision?

•
Does any available next investigation have enough expected decision impact to justify continuing?

•
Are contradictions resolved or explicitly carried into the decision?

•
Have investigation bounds been reached?

•
If evidence is insufficient, can the system safely stop and request/recommend further review rather than invent certainty?

9. Critical Precautions

•
Never hallucinate data or graph relationships.

•
Never fabricate benchmark accuracy or hard-code expected answers.

•
Do not treat a risk score as fraud truth unless the dataset defines it that way.

•
Do not treat historical cases as automatic labels.

•
Do not confuse confidence with certainty.

•
Do not invent policy or regulatory thresholds.

•
Keep recommendation, authorization and execution separate.

•
Bound investigation to prevent loops and low-value exploration.

•
Do not add technology merely because it sounds advanced.

•
Do not optimize only for the demo; evaluate real benchmark cases.

•
Expose contradictions, insufficient evidence, tool failure and authorization failure.

•
Do not make the UI the product; make the investigation mechanism the product.

11. 3–5 Minute Demo Strategy

1 Trigger one benchmark investigation case.

2 Show initial uncertainty and competing hypotheses.

3 Show a meaningful TigerGraph relationship/pattern.

4 Show the information gap.

5 Show candidate next investigations.

6 Show why the selected investigation can affect the decision.

7 Show contradiction if available.

8 Show state update.

9 Show STOP or justified continuation.

10 Show recommendation/approval route without collapsing authorization into execution.

11 Trace the final conclusion back to graph evidence.

Demo goal: make the judge see that the agent decided what it needed to know next—not merely that it retrieved

graph data.

12. How the Final Build Prompt Should Be Produced

Do not write the final coding prompt first. The build prompt should be generated only after the investigation mechanism

survives review.

Pipeline: Challenge PDF → Investigation State → Next-Evidence Mechanism → STOP Mechanism → Skeptical

Red-Team → Real Benchmark Cases → Formal Specification → Technology Mapping → Implementation

Specification → Final Build Prompt

•
Work sequentially and STOP after each design phase.

•
Use actual benchmark cases before freezing the mechanism.

•
Formalize state, evidence, hypotheses, candidate investigations, decision impact, selection, STOP, safety and evaluation.

•
Only then map responsibilities to TigerGraph/GSQL/GraphRAG/MCP/LLM/memory/UI.

•
Only then generate the final implementation/build prompt.

Build-prompt safeguards: the coding AI must not silently replace TigerGraph, invent schema, fabricate evidence,

remove STOP logic, turn adaptive investigation into a fixed workflow, or allow unauthorized execution. Any forced

change must be explicitly reported with constraint, proposed change, reason, impact and alternative.

14. Recommended Work Sequence From Here

1 Do not code yet. Finalize the three mechanisms conceptually.

2 Read the README and dataset schema; never invent schema from the brief.

3 Take 3–5 actual benchmark cases and manually perform the investigation process.

4 Write your own reasoning first: hypotheses, unknowns, contradictions, next useful evidence and stopping point.

5 Give your reasoning to the AI and ask it to attack/formalize it—not simply agree.

6 Red-team the control loop.

7 Define measurable next-evidence and STOP mechanisms.

8 Freeze the investigation specification.

9 Map responsibilities to technology and remove anything without a necessary job.

10 Generate the final implementation/build prompt.

11 Implement in small verifiable slices.

12 Evaluate on the benchmark cases.

13 Only then optimize demo polish.

16. Final North-Star Principle

Weak: “We built an AI fraud agent using TigerGraph, GraphRAG, MCP and an LLM.”

Stronger proposed direction: “We designed a measurable, evidence-grounded investigation process that

determines what it needs to know, chooses the next investigation because it can affect the decision, updates its

reasoning when evidence changes, recognizes when further investigation is not justified, and produces a

policy-compliant, traceable outcome.”

This is our current north star. It is a proposed design direction, not an official challenge quote.
